How to use this list

Every project below gives you the file, the stock, the bits, a carve time, and the one thing it teaches. Three rules make the list work:

  • Do them in order. Each one assumes the lesson from the one before. Skipping to the face is how people get the sad result.
  • Carve times are for a hobby machine with a roughing pass and a sensible stepover, which our carve-time guide explains. Without a roughing pass, treble them.
  • Finish every one. Even the flower. Raw wood is one colour and only shadow gives it shape; the finishing guide is the other half of every project here. The quick version for small pieces: brush the fuzz off, seal, wipe a dark glaze back off the high points, oil.

1. A single flower

File: {{TITLE:leaf}}, {{PRICE:leaf}}
Stock: basswood or poplar offcut, 150 by 100 mm, 3/4 inch.
Bits: 1/4 inch end mill to rough, 2 mm tapered ball to finish.
Time: 30 to 45 minutes.
What it teaches: the whole workflow, start to finish, with nothing that can look "wrong".

Magnolia blossoms are the perfect first carve because petals forgive everything. A slightly coarse stepover reads as texture. A little fuzz brushes off. There are no eyes to line up. Set the finish pass at 12%, let the machine run, and use the time to read the finishing guide. When it is done, brush, oil, and put it on a shelf. You now own a carving and you have learned half of what this craft is.

2. A small heart panel

File: {{TITLE:heart}}, {{PRICE:heart}}
Stock: cherry or walnut, 200 by 200 mm, 3/4 inch.
Bits: 1/4 inch end mill, 2 mm tapered ball.
Time: 45 to 70 minutes.
What it teaches: hardwood, and the difference finishing makes.

Your first hardwood. Cherry and walnut cut cleaner than basswood, hold an edge, and take oil like nothing else. A heart made of butterflies and flowers has enough small elements to show you what a 2 mm bit can and cannot resolve, and it is the first piece on this list somebody will actually want as a gift. It also prints and lasers, which makes it the one you can make on almost anything. Glaze this one; the contrast between the dark ground and the pale butterflies is the lesson.

3. A single animal portrait

File: {{TITLE:portrait}}, {{PRICE:portrait}}
Stock: walnut or maple, 250 by 300 mm, 1 inch.
Bits: 1/4 inch end mill, 1 mm tapered ball.
Time: 1.5 to 2.5 hours.
What it teaches: fur, and why stepover matters.

The wolf is the first project where the bit marks will show if you rush. Fur pushes the finish pass into territory where a 15% stepover reads as corduroy from across the room. Tighten it to 10% on the wolf itself and leave the moon and sky at 15%, using a boundary vector, and you have just learned the single most useful trick in relief carving. Carve the moon in maple if you want it to glow pale against a dark glaze.

4. A wreath

File: {{TITLE:wreath}}, {{PRICE:wreath}}
Stock: cherry, 300 by 300 mm, 1 inch.
Bits: 1/4 inch end mill, 1.5 mm tapered ball.
Time: 2 to 3 hours.
What it teaches: overlapping elements and depth layers.

Leaves crossing under each other, antlers over the leaves, a stag in the middle. A wreath is where you find out that relief depth is layered, not smooth, and that the way the layers read after carving depends on the glaze getting down into the crossings. Slow the feed a little on the finish pass, 3,000 mm/min rather than 4,000, and the crossings stay crisp.

5. A coastal scene

File: {{TITLE:coast}}, {{PRICE:coast}}
Stock: walnut or sapele, 350 by 250 mm, 1 inch.
Bits: 1/4 inch end mill, 1 mm tapered ball.
Time: 2.5 to 4 hours.
What it teaches: foreground, middle ground, background.

The first multi-element scene: a lighthouse in front of water in front of sky and a sunburst. This is where relief starts to feel like sculpture rather than decoration, and it is the project that shows you why depth has to scale with width. Crush the Z on a scene like this and the layers stack instead of receding. Keep the file's proportions and it reads from across the room.

6. A name sign with one relief element

File: {{TITLE:sign}}, {{PRICE:sign}}

Bits: 60 degree V-bit for the lettering, 1.5 mm tapered ball for the relief.
Time: 1 to 1.5 hours.
What it teaches: two toolpaths on one board, and personalising.

Relief plus V-carved text on the same panel. The relief goes first, the lettering second as its own toolpath, and the two have to share a zero. This is the combination behind almost every commission you will ever be asked for: a name, a date, and something carved beside it. Practise it here on a design that is already a gift. Swap "DAD" for any name in your CAM and you have your first custom order.

7. A pet piece

File: {{TITLE:pet}}, {{PRICE:pet}}
Stock: cherry or walnut, 300 by 250 mm, 1 inch.
Bits: 1/4 inch end mill, 1 mm tapered ball, 0.5 mm tapered ball for the paw pads.
Time: 3 to 4 hours.
What it teaches: a boundary for the fine bit, and carving something that matters to someone.

A human hand cradling a dog's paw. It is a memorial piece more often than not, and the first project here where the result will be given to someone who is grieving. Carve it with care: the hand at 1 mm, then a boundary around the paw for the 0.5 mm pass so the pads read. Do not attempt a custom photo-to-relief of somebody's actual dog as your first pet piece; carve this designed file first, so you know your machine can do it before you promise anyone anything.

8. A dark-themed panel

File: {{TITLE:gothic}}, {{PRICE:gothic}}
Stock: walnut, or cherry with a black glaze, 300 by 400 mm, 1 inch.
Bits: 1/4 inch end mill, 1 mm tapered ball.
Time: 2.5 to 4 hours.
What it teaches: contrast as a design tool.

A raven under a full moon. Gothic pieces are satisfying because the contrast between a pale moon and a near-black bird does most of the work for you, and it is the project where the glaze step stops being a finishing detail and becomes the design. Wipe the moon almost clean; leave the raven and the forest dark. Skip the gloss. A satin or matte topcoat keeps the wood reading dark and moody.

9. A face

File: {{TITLE:faith}}, {{PRICE:faith}}
Stock: cherry, 300 by 450 mm, 1 inch. Not oak.
Bits: 1/4 inch end mill, 1 mm tapered ball, 0.5 mm tapered ball inside a boundary around the face.
Time: 5 to 8 hours.
What it teaches: everything at once.

Faces are the hardest thing in relief carving. Likeness lives in the eyelids and the corners of the mouth, and those are the smallest features on the panel. This is why the face is number nine and not number one. By now you know the boundary trick, you know why the bit has to be smaller than the feature, you know the glaze will give the eyes their shadow, and you know not to carve it in oak. The Face of Christ is the most carved devotional piece we make, and the messages people send after giving one are the reason this list exists.

10. A large landscape

File: {{TITLE:landscape}}, {{PRICE:landscape}}
Stock: walnut or cherry, 500 by 300 mm, 1 inch.
Bits: 1/4 inch end mill, 1/8 inch tapered ball for the semi-finish, 1 mm tapered ball inside a boundary around the eagle.
Time: 4 to 7 hours with the full bit ladder. Overnight without it.
What it teaches: the full three-pass method, and patience.

By project ten you are ready for a multi-pass carve that runs while you sleep. Sky, mountains, trees and a bird at four distinct depths, plus a second eagle in flight. Rough it with the flat cutter, semi-finish everything at 15% with the 1/8 inch, then draw a vector around the eagle's head and wings and run the 1 mm inside it only. Plan the toolpaths carefully and check the zero twice: a 500 mm walnut blank is a serious board, and you do not want to learn at hour six that the origin was wrong.

Questions people ask

What is the easiest relief carving for a complete beginner?

A single flower or leaf in basswood. Petals forgive a coarse stepover and there are no faces to get wrong.

Which wood should a beginner use?

Basswood or poplar for the first one, cherry from the second onward. Avoid oak and pine for anything with fine detail.

How long does a beginner carving take?

Under an hour for the first two projects, two to four hours through the middle of the list, and an afternoon or overnight for the final landscape.

What bits do I need to start?

A 1/4 inch end mill and a 2 mm tapered ball nose will do the first two projects. Add a 1 mm and a 0.5 mm tapered ball as you go up the list.

Why is the face so far down the list?

Because likeness lives in the smallest features on the panel and needs every lesson above it: a fine bit inside a boundary, the right wood, depth kept in proportion, and a glaze to give the eyes their shadow.

Do I have to finish them?

Yes. Raw wood is one colour. The glaze-and-oil step is what turns a carving that looks flat off the machine into one that reads across a room, and it is where most of the difference between a beginner and a professional result actually lives.
