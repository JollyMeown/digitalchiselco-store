The short answer: on a typical 300 mm (12 in) relief, a 3 mm ball nose finishes 97 to 99 percent of the surface to within a quarter of a millimetre of the model. A 1.5 mm ball nose only earns its keep on the last 1 to 3 percent: the gaps between feathers, the corner of an eye, the space under a claw. Running the 1.5 mm over the whole panel doubles your finishing time to get that last bit. Running it only where the 3 mm could not reach, which is what rest machining does, gets the same detail in about half the time: 42 to 49 percent less finishing time on the five real designs we simulated.

That is the whole article in one paragraph. The rest shows how we measured it, what the difference actually looks like on a carving, and how to set up rest machining in Aspire, VCarve Pro, Fusion, Carveco and Carbide Create.

How we measured it

These are simulations, not stopwatch times from a machine, and we want to be exact about what that means.

  • Each design is the real STL file you download, run through the same engine as our free Will it cut? checker, on a grid of 0.17 mm.
  • The cutter is modelled as a true hemisphere. For every point on the surface the engine works out the lowest the ball's tip can go without the ball hitting the carving anywhere, and what surface the ball leaves behind. The gap between that and the model is the detail it misses.
  • We checked the engine against a shape with a known answer: in a 90 degree V-groove a ball of radius r stops r(√2 - 1) above the bottom. It matches to a few hundredths of a millimetre.
  • Finishing time is a raster pass at 10 percent stepover on a mid-size hobby machine (the Shapeoko, X-Carve and Onefinity class) at the feeds we publish on every product page. Roughing is the same whichever finishing plan you pick, about 1.2 to 1.6 hours here, so it is left out of the comparisons.
  • What it does not model: holder collisions, deflection, and the air moves a rest pass makes between islands. So treat the times as honest estimates, and expect a real rest pass to run a little longer than shown.

Rest machining: the small bit only where it is needed

Rest machining means running a second, smaller finishing tool only over the material the first tool could not reach, instead of over the whole part again. The software already knows where the 3 mm fell short, because it computed that toolpath. So the 1.5 mm pass becomes a short cleanup of the deep, narrow places rather than a second ten-hour raster.

There are two ways to tell the software where those places are:

  • Automatic: the software calculates the unreached areas itself and sends the small bit only there. Aspire, VCarve Pro and Fusion can do this.
  • By vector: you draw a boundary around the areas you care about, and the small-bit finishing pass is limited to the inside of it. Every program here can do this, and it is the only option in Carveco's relief toolpath and in Carbide Create.

On a busy, all-over design like this one, automatic rest machining is clearly the better tool. The vector method shines when the fine detail lives in one place: a face, a pair of eyes, claws, lettering.

At 450 mm (18 in) the picture is the same, 44 to 48 percent saved, because every feature grows with the panel and the 3 mm fits into more of them. Carve smaller, and the balance tips the other way: the same details shrink, the 3 mm misses more, and the 1.5 mm pass earns more of its time.

Setting it up in your software

The idea is the same everywhere: finish the whole model with the 3 mm first, then add a second finishing pass with the 1.5 mm that only runs where it is needed. Keep the same stock setup and the same XY origin for both, and after the tool change re-zero Z for the new bit (or let your tool setter do it).

Vectric Aspire and VCarve Pro

  • In current versions, the 3D Finish toolpath accepts more than one tool. Add the 3 mm ball nose, then the 1.5 mm below it. With more than one tool in the list, the toolpath rest-machines automatically: each tool only cuts what the one before it could not reach.
  • The Minimum detail slider decides how small an unreached area can be before the small tool ignores it. Raise it and the rest pass gets shorter; lower it and it chases every speck.
  • To limit the small bit to part of the model, set the Machining Limit Boundary to Selected Vector(s) and select vectors you have drawn around the detail areas.
  • If your version does not offer a second tool in the 3D Finish toolpath, use the vector method below: a normal 3D finish with the 1.5 mm, limited to selected vectors.

Fusion

  • Finish with the 3 mm (Parallel or Scallop), then add a second finishing operation with the 1.5 mm and turn on Rest Machining, with the source set to your previous operations, so it only cuts what they left.
  • For the deep creases between forms, a Pencil toolpath with the small bit is made for exactly this cleanup.
  • Use a machining boundary if you also want to keep it to one area.

Carveco

  • Carveco's automatic rest machining is documented for 2D area clearance; for reliefs, use vectors. Draw closed vectors around the detail areas, select them, and run a second Machine Relief finishing pass with the 1.5 mm limited to the selected vector.

Carbide Create Pro

  • Carbide Create does not rest-machine 3D toolpaths automatically. Draw vectors around the areas that need the small bit, and select them for a second 3D finishing toolpath with the 1.5 mm.

For the small bit itself, a tapered 1.5 mm ball nose is the better buy than a straight one: the cone makes it far stiffer, so it reaches down into narrow gaps without flexing or snapping. Starting speeds, feeds and stepovers are in our tapered ball nose guide.

Common mistakes

  • Running the 1.5 mm over everything "to be safe". It doubles the finishing time to improve 1 to 3 percent of the surface.
  • Lowering the stepover to get back missing detail. Stepover only changes the ridges left between passes. At 10 percent those ridges are under a hundredth of a millimetre for both bits, far below what sanding removes. Detail a ball cannot reach stays unreached at any stepover; only a smaller ball gets in.
  • Using a straight 1.5 mm bit in deep gaps. A long, thin, straight shank flexes. A tapered ball nose has the same tip on a much stiffer body.
  • Moving the XY origin between the two passes. The rest pass is only useful if it lines up with the first finish. Keep the origin; re-zero Z only.
  • Judging the result before the finish goes on. A dark glaze settles into exactly the narrow places a 3 mm could not quite reach, which is why a 3 mm carving often looks sharper finished than raw. Our finishing guide shows how.

Questions

Is a 1.5 mm ball nose worth buying?

Yes, as a second finishing bit rather than your only one. Used for a rest pass after a 3 mm, it adds 7 to 47 minutes on a 300 mm panel and brings back the narrow detail. Used over the whole panel, it doubles your finishing time for very little extra.

Does rest machining work in VCarve Pro, or only in Aspire?

In current versions, both: the 3D Finish toolpath rest-machines when you give it more than one tool, and both can limit a toolpath to selected vectors. Fusion does it too. In Carveco's relief toolpath and in Carbide Create you use the vector method.

What stepover should I use?

8 to 12 percent of the tool diameter is the usual range for 3D finishing, and we used 10 percent for every number here. At 10 percent the ridges left by either bit are too small to see after sanding, so there is little to gain from going lower.

How long does the rest pass itself take?

On these five 300 mm designs, 7 to 47 minutes, because the 1.5 mm only covers 1 to 8 percent of the panel.

Can I check my own file before I carve it?

Yes. Drop any STL into Will it cut? and it shows what each ball size misses on your file, at the size you plan to carve. It runs in your browser, so the file never leaves your computer.
