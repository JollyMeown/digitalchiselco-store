The short answer: for a 3 mm ball nose on a relief you will oil straight off the machine, use 8 to 10 percent stepover. If you will brush and lightly sand before finishing, 15 percent is plenty, and it cuts finishing time by a third. Up to 25 percent leaves ridges that a pass of 220 grit still removes, in under half the time of 10 percent. Past that, the ridges become real work. Stepover buys time with ridges, and nothing else: it never brings back detail the ball cannot reach.

What stepover actually is

A 3D finishing pass does not carve the whole surface at once. The ball nose runs back and forth in straight lines, and each line sits a little over from the last. That sideways step between neighbouring passes is the stepover. Software usually asks for it as a percentage of the tool diameter, so 10 percent on a 3 mm ball means the passes are 0.3 mm apart.

Why it leaves ridges, and how tall

Each pass of a round tip cuts a shallow groove. Where two grooves meet, a thin ridge of wood is left standing between them: machinists call it a scallop or cusp. Its height is plain geometry, h = r - √(r² - (s/2)²), for a ball of radius r at stepover s. The useful consequence: double the stepover and the ridge gets about four times taller, while the finishing time halves.

To judge whether a ridge matters, compare it with what a pass of 220 grit takes off, about 0.05 mm. A ridge well under that is gone the moment you sand. A ridge near it needs a proper sanding. A ridge well over it is texture you will be fighting.

Bigger ball, finer stepover

The same percentage does not mean the same finish across bit sizes. At 10 percent, a 1.5 mm ball leaves 0.004 mm ridges, a 3 mm ball 0.008 mm and a 6 mm ball 0.015 mm, because the passes are further apart on the bigger ball. Turn it around, and a bigger ball can reach the same ridge height with fewer passes:

1.5 mm ball30%3.7 h0.035 mm

So the 6 mm ball is the fastest way to a sandable finish, and that is exactly the trap: it cannot get into fine detail at any stepover. On the designs we tested it missed visible detail on 3 to 9 percent of every panel. The 3 mm is the best all-round finishing bit for relief work, and a 1.5 mm rest pass handles the few places it cannot reach.

Five things that matter more than people think

  • Run the passes along the grain. Most software has a raster angle. Lined up with the grain, the ridges hide in the wood's own lines; across it, they catch the light.
  • Stepover never recovers detail. If the eye or a feather looks soft, a finer stepover will not fix it. A smaller ball will.
  • Hang it where the light rakes. A carving by a window or under a picture light shows ridges that disappear under flat ceiling light. Finish for the wall it is going on.
  • Oil makes ridges look taller. Oil soaks into the ridge sides and darkens them. If you oil without sanding, go finer, not coarser.
  • Check the time before you commit. Our Will it cut? tool shows the finishing time and the fastest sand-free stepover for your own file at your own size, right in your browser.

Questions

What stepover should I use for a 3 mm (1/8 in) ball nose?

8 to 10 percent if you will not sand, 12 to 15 percent if you will brush and glaze, and up to 25 percent on surfaces you will sand properly with 220 grit. A 1/8 inch ball is 3.175 mm, close enough that the same percentages apply.

Does a smaller stepover give more detail?

No. It gives smaller ridges between passes. The detail you get is set by how small the ball is, because a ball cannot reach into a gap narrower than itself at any stepover.

Should I set stepover as a percentage or in millimetres?

Either works as long as you remember it is the distance between passes that matters. A percentage is handy because the ridge height stays the same fraction of the ball's size, so 10 percent looks alike on any ball. In millimetres, the same stepover leaves smaller ridges on a bigger ball.

How much time does going from 10 to 15 percent save?

A third of the finishing time. On the bass at 300 mm that is 5.5 hours down to 3.7 hours; at 450 mm, 12.5 hours down to 8.3.

Why can I still see lines after finishing?

Usually because the ridges are bigger than the sanding took off, the light is raking across them, and oil has darkened their sides. Brush or sand once more across the lines, or finish the next one at a finer stepover.
